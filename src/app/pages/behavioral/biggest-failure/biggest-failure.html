Tell Me About Your Biggest Failure

Frame the answer with STAR + a lesson — and a full model answer for a real engineering failure.

Do not memorize every sentence. Remember one skeleton and fill it with your own story:

  1. Situation — redesigned a complex offer system → 1.5-month deadline.
  2. Complexity — order / line item / brand / category / item + customer history + wallet + discounts + free items + combinations / exclusions.
  3. Failure — underestimated the interaction complexity and committed to the architecture and timeline too early.
  4. Impact — application / evaluation logic became complicated and harder to extend and scale.
  5. Learning — for complex rule systems, understand the dimensions and interactions first; separate evaluation from application; validate with complex scenarios before committing.
One-line answer: “I committed to a timeline before fully understanding how dozens of offer rules would interact — I take responsibility, and it changed how I estimate any rule-heavy system.”
  1. They want proof you have committed a mistake in the past — which means you are open to learning new things. Someone who claims “no mistakes” is usually not open to growing or changing.
  2. They check your self-awareness — if you at least know what the mistake was and remember it, they can trust you will not repeat it.
  1. Make it reasonable. It must be a mistake anyone could make — never a “stupid” mistake. Good examples: underestimating a scope/change, over-committing to a time period, or agreeing to implement a functionality you do not know yet.
  2. Own what you did wrong. Say clearly “I did X wrong” and take responsibility. Never blame teammates, the PM, or the business.
  3. Show the learning. State what you learned and — more importantly — what you do differently now, so you will not make the same mistake again.
  • Use “I” statements and own the mistake inside the first 20 seconds — hesitation reads as an excuse.
  • Weight the ending: roughly 60–70% of the answer on ownership and the lesson, not on the problem details.
  • Keep it to 60–90 seconds. Pause between boxes; do not rush.
  • Bridge to the role: “I now do X, which is exactly how I would approach this problem here.”
1 · SITUATION (15 s)

One of my biggest failures as an engineer was during the redesign of an offer system. We had a timeline of around one and a half months, and I initially underestimated the complexity of the problem. I believed that if we designed the different offer types properly, we could implement them within that timeline.

2 · COMPLEXITY (20 s)

The actual requirements were quite complex. An offer could be based on order value, line items, brand, category, or individual items. There could also be conditions such as must-buy rules, customer purchase history, customer activity, wallet points, instant discounts, or free items. On top of that, some offers could be combined with others, while some were mutually exclusive.

3 · FAILURE — what I did wrong (15 s)

My mistake was that I focused too much on implementing the individual offer rules and didn’t spend enough time upfront understanding the interaction between all these rules. In particular, I didn’t properly separate the different stages of the system — defining an offer, determining whether a customer was eligible, evaluating competing offers, and finally applying the benefits to an order.

I take responsibility for that. The requirements were complex, but I should have recognized that complexity earlier instead of committing to the timeline before fully decomposing the problem.

4 · IMPACT (10 s)

As we moved closer to launch, this became a problem. The application logic became increasingly complicated because it had to consider customer history, order context, and interactions between multiple offers. The system became harder to extend and scale than I had originally anticipated.

5 · LEARNING (30 s)

The biggest lesson I learned was that in a rule-heavy system, complexity doesn’t come only from the number of features. It comes from the interaction between different rules and different dimensions of context.

Since then, when I work on a complex backend system, I first identify the dimensions of variability and their interactions. I separate evaluation from execution, identify dependencies and failure scenarios, and validate the design against a few complex real-world scenarios before committing to the implementation timeline.

That experience changed the way I approach architecture and estimation. As an engineer becomes more senior, it is not enough to ask, “Can I implement these requirements?” I also need to ask, “Have I understood the complexity well enough to commit to this design and timeline?”

✔ Do✘ Don’t
Pick a real, work-related failure — moderate severity, believable. Pick a fake, trivial, or “stupid” mistake — it kills credibility.
Take full responsibility: “I should have recognized…”, “I take responsibility…”. Blame teammates, the PM, or the business for the outcome.
Explain the root cause — why you underestimated (e.g. counted features, not interactions). Make the failure catastrophic (firing, big data loss) without a strong recovery framing.
Spend most of the answer on the lesson and the behaviour change. Spend most of the answer on the problem — the lesson needs the airtime.
Quantify the impact: timeline, scale, cost, complexity growth. Say “I have no big failure” or “I can’t think of one”.
Mention the process you now follow and tie it to this job. End without the lesson — that turns the answer into a red flag.

► Say this

  • “I take responsibility for that.”
  • “I should have recognized the complexity earlier.”
  • “I committed to the timeline before fully decomposing the problem.”
  • “Since then, when I work on a complex system I…” (concrete new behaviour).
  • “I now ask: have I understood the complexity well enough to commit?”

► Avoid this

  • “The requirements kept changing.”
  • “The manager forced the deadline.”
  • “The team didn’t tell me earlier.”
  • “It was a stupid typo / I was careless” — unprofessional framing.
  • “I have never made a big mistake.”
Golden rule: whatever happened, the interviewer should hear one “I” taking ownership and three “now I…” sentences showing the change.
