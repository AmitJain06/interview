Tell Me About a Major Production Incident

Own a P0 with a four-phase response — prep, detect, contain, learn. Structure over panic, ownership over blame, system change over one-off fixes.

Do not memorize every sentence. Remember four phases and fill them with your own story:

  1. Preparation — know the policy before anything breaks: “any user must be able to log in by OTP; the OTP is delivered by SMS or WhatsApp; max 3 retries is sufficient.” This baseline is what “working” means.
  2. Detection & Analysis — P1 reported (driver app) → confirmed WhatsApp works → 30 min later all three apps escalate → P0 → no deployments, nothing changed → logs insufficient → data-platform login-success query → provider portal → DLT blocked → fresh DLT registration takes ~4 days.
  3. Containment, Eradication & Recovery — WhatsApp is healthy but only some apps had the retry flag; no feature flag, no fallback → ship a config-driven route behind a feature flag → users log in again, success rate back to baseline.
  4. Post-Incident Activity — re-apply for DLT, make the comms service configurable, add feature flags and fallbacks for critical flows, remove hardcoded codes from config, write the postmortem.
One-line answer: “A P0 login outage: a blocked telecom DLT registration silently killed every SMS OTP across our buyer, driver and vendor apps. I drove a four-phase response — detected it with data in about 30 minutes, restored login with a feature-flagged WhatsApp fallback, and rebuilt the OTP path to be config-driven with automatic fallbacks.”
  1. They want to see how you behave under pressure — do you panic and guess, or follow a method?
  2. They assess ownership — were you driving the incident, or just tangentially involved?
  3. They assess engineering judgement — how did you isolate a failure that produced no obvious error?
  4. They assess growth — did you change the system, or just patch the symptom?
  1. Structure with the four-phase model. Naming “Preparation → Detection & Analysis → Containment, Eradication & Recovery → Post-Incident Activity” instantly signals senior incident ownership — do not tell it as a confused story.
  2. Prove method, not luck. At each step say what you ruled out: “nothing deployed → not a regression”, “WhatsApp OTP worked → auth is fine, delivery only”, “provider portal → DLT blocked”.
  3. End on the system-level lesson. Feature flags, config, fallbacks, monitoring — then bridge to their team: “which is exactly how I’d handle incident response here.”
  • Name the severity up front: “raised as P1, promoted to P0 within 30 minutes.”
  • Say “I was the on-call owner” inside the first 20 seconds — it locks in ownership for the whole answer.
  • Keep it to 60–90 seconds. Pause between phases; never rush the post-incident part.
  • End on the change: “Since then the comms service is configurable, with fallbacks — and provider delivery is monitored.”
0 · SETUP — who I am, what the system is (10 s)

I work on the identity and login platform of a commerce marketplace with three apps — buyer, driver, and vendor portal. Login is OTP-based: a user requests an OTP, receives it by SMS as the primary channel or WhatsApp as the alternate, and completes login within three attempts.

1 · PREPARATION — the policy that defined “working” (10 s)

Our standing policy was simple: every user must be able to log in with an OTP; the OTP is delivered by SMS or WhatsApp; max 3 retries is sufficient to log in. That baseline mattered during the incident — it let us split “auth is broken” from “delivery is broken” in minutes instead of hours.

2 · DETECTION & ANALYSIS (25 s)

L2 raised the issue as a P1 — users could not log in to the driver app. Because the impact looked narrow, we concentrated on that area first; that assumption cost us time. We asked the reporter to try a WhatsApp OTP and it worked, which immediately ruled out the auth pipeline — the problem was delivery, not login itself.

Within about 30 minutes, escalations came in from the buyer app and the vendor portal too, so the issue was promoted to P0. As the owner, I first confirmed the obvious: nothing had been deployed and nothing in the code had changed — so this was not a regression from a release. Our server logs were not sufficient — they did not capture the provider’s response. So I queried the data platform for successful logins over the last couple of hours, and the success rate had dropped sharply. That pointed at OTP delivery.

On the SMS provider’s portal I found the root cause: our DLT registration was blocked. DLT — the telecom registry that validates SMS senders — was rejecting every request, so every SMS OTP was silently dropped at the operator. And a fresh DLT registration takes about four days, so waiting was not an option.

3 · CONTAINMENT, ERADICATION & RECOVERY (20 s)

WhatsApp was still working, but only some flows had the WhatsApp retry flag — there was no feature flag and no automatic fallback in the OTP path. So we made one safe change behind a feature flag: the OTP channel became config-driven — prefer SMS when the DLT is healthy, fall back to WhatsApp, and always allow a retry via WhatsApp. We enabled it progressively per app, watching the login success rate as we flipped. Users could log in again within a few hours, and the success rate returned to baseline. Because it stayed behind the flag, we could toggle it without another deploy.

4 · POST-INCIDENT ACTIVITY (15 s)

After the incident we fixed the system, not just the symptom: we applied for the DLT again and corrected the compliance issue; we made the comms service fully configurable — provider and channel per template and per app now live in configuration, not code; we standardized feature flags for critical flows; we wrote automatic fallbacks for important flows, SMS to WhatsApp to in-app OTP; and we removed hardcoded codes like sender IDs and retry counts from the codebase.

We also closed the detection gap that had cost us time: provider response codes and delivery status are now logged, and an alert fires when the login success rate drops — so the next time this class of failure starts, we get a dashboard alert instead of a P0.

5 · THE LESSON (15 s)

The biggest lesson: when your critical path crosses a third-party dependency, incident readiness is about knowing the provider’s failure modes, having a fallback built into the flow — not a hack that lives in one app — and monitoring at the boundary of that dependency, not just in your own logs. I now ask one question on every design I touch: “if this provider goes down tomorrow, can the user still complete this flow?” And I start triage by checking data and provider status, not only application logs.

Adapt, don’t copy: keep your own timeline and numbers honest, but keep the skeleton — preparation, detection, containment, recovery, post-incident — and the demo of method: at every step, say what you ruled out.

An interviewer who has been through incidents themselves will probe the details below. You do not have to narrate every one — but you must know them, because they are exactly the parts people miss when they tell this story.

You will use these words in the answer, so be ready to explain any of them if asked. One-answer definitions:

  • P1 / P0Priority of the ticket: P1 = impact contained, resolve within the SLA window (commonly ~24 h). P0 = service down or broad impact — immediate attention and escalation (often 2–4 h).
  • DLTDistributed Ledger Technology — the telecom registry (mandated by TRAI in India) that validates SMS sender headers and content templates before an operator delivers anything.
  • Feature flagA runtime configuration toggle that changes behaviour without a deployment — enables progressive rollout and instant rollback.
  • FallbackAn alternate path invoked automatically when the primary one fails — here SMS → WhatsApp → in-app OTP.
  • Data platformThe analytics/warehouse layer you query for behaviour (here: login success rate) when service logs alone are not enough.
  • Circuit breakerA pattern that stops hammering a failing dependency and instead rotates channels or degrades gracefully.
✔ Do✘ Don’t
Lead with severity and impact: all three apps, P1 raised then P0. Start with “users couldn’t log in” and wander into timeline trivia.
Narrate method — name what each check ruled out: deploy history, WhatsApp test, provider portal. Skip the analysis and jump to “so we changed the config”.
Own your specific actions as the on-call owner, and use “we” only for team decisions. Say “the team did” everything — the interviewer can’t see you in the story.
Give both the one-off fix (flag + WhatsApp fallback) and the system fix (config, fallbacks, monitoring, DLT re-applied). End at “users could log in again” — recovery without hardening sounds like luck.
Quantify honestly: ~30 min to root cause, a few hours to restored login, success rate back to baseline. Invent precise uptime/revenue numbers, or inflate the outage duration.
Close with the lesson and tie it to their team: “which is how I’d run incident response here.” End without the lesson, or blame the provider/L2 for the detection delay.

However polished the main answer, an interviewer who has lived through outages will dig into the same spots. Prepare these — each has “why they ask”, “how to answer”, and a sample line to adapt.

  • “What is DLT, and why did the block break SMS but not WhatsApp?”
    • Why they ask: they want proof you understand your own root cause, not just that you read it off a portal.
    • How to answer: explain the validation step, then the channel difference in one breath.
    • Sample line: “DLT — Distributed Ledger Technology — is the telecom registry that validates SMS senders: every message must match a registered header and an approved template. When our registration was blocked, the operator dropped every SMS. WhatsApp is an OTT channel that bypasses DLT, so those OTPs still arrived.”
  • “Why didn’t you notice sooner? What was the monitoring gap?”
    • Why they ask: detection time is the real grading curve in incidents; they want self-awareness.
    • How to answer: own the gap, name the exact missing signal, and state the fix.
    • Sample line: “Our logs showed the OTP as sent, but we never logged the provider’s response or delivery status, so a provider-side block looked normal. That’s the gap we closed — delivery status logged and an alert on login-success drops.”
  • “What was your personal role in this incident?”
    • Why they ask: ownership — they need to see you driving, not attending.
    • How to answer: one line of role, then a short list of concrete actions; split “I” (execution) from “we” (decisions).
    • Sample line: “I was the on-call owner from P1 to closure: I confirmed nothing was deployed, pulled the login-success series from the data platform, found the DLT block on the provider portal, and drove the feature-flagged fallback and its staged rollout.”
  • “Why didn’t you just switch everyone to WhatsApp immediately?”
    • Why they ask: they want to hear risk judgement under pressure, not heroics.
    • How to answer: name why a blanket change was risky and how the flag kept the change small, reversible, and observable.
    • Sample line: “The retry-via-WhatsApp path existed only in some flows — flipping everyone meant deploying to three apps during an outage. That’s exactly when you don’t make broad changes. We made routing config-driven behind a flag and enabled it per app while watching the success rate.”
  • “What would you have done if WhatsApp had also failed?”
    • Why they ask: failure trees — can you design resilience, or only follow a script?
    • How to answer: give a layered fallback chain and one degradation/grace idea.
    • Sample line: “In-app/email OTP as a third channel, a second SMS gateway with pre-approved templates, a circuit breaker that rotates channels after repeated failures — and for the extreme case, session grace so recently authenticated users keep working while we restore delivery.”
  • “How long was the outage and how many users were affected?”
    • Why they ask: impact sizing and honesty under pressure.
    • How to answer: rounded, honest magnitudes; translate impact into user/business terms.
    • Sample line: “Order of tens of thousands of users across the three apps; login success fell sharply and returned to baseline a few hours after the flag was enabled. The business impact was real: drivers couldn’t go online for work and vendors couldn’t manage orders.”
  • “Why didn’t you have a feature flag before, and would one have prevented this?”
    • Why they ask: they want precision about what tools do and don’t solve.
    • How to answer: define the flag, be honest that it wouldn’t have stopped the DLT block, and show what it does change.
    • Sample line: “A feature flag is a runtime toggle that changes behaviour without a deploy. It wouldn’t have prevented the DLT block — but it would have turned a multi-hour recovery into a minutes-long toggle with instant rollback.”
  • “How did you communicate during the incident?”
    • Why they ask: tactical communication is a senior skill; they want to see you managed stakeholders, not just code.
    • How to answer: name the channel, the cadence, and who got what.
    • Sample line: “A named incident channel for the team, short status updates to L2 and stakeholders at checkpoints instead of constant chatter, user-facing messaging on the affected apps, and a written postmortem with timeline, root cause, and action items after closure.”
  • “If you could change one thing about how this was handled, what would it be?”
    • Why they ask: judgement — can you find the highest-leverage improvement?
    • How to answer: pick one thing, say why it outranks the others, and connect it to a system change.
    • Sample line: “Detection speed. The recovery itself was clean, but finding the failure took too long because delivery wasn’t observable. We closed that with provider response logging and a success-rate alert — the same class of problem now shows up as a dashboard alert, not a P0.”
  • “Was the initial P1 a mistake? Why wasn’t it P0 from the start?”
    • Why they ask: they want to see fair, systems thinking — not blame of L2.
    • How to answer: defend the escalation path, then show what should have been better.
    • Sample line: “No — P1 was right for a single-app report with a 24-hour SLA. It became P0 the moment escalations showed all three apps. The process worked; what we improved is earlier verification of impact instead of scoping from one report.”

► Say this

  • “I was the on-call owner.”
  • “Nothing was deployed and nothing had changed, so I looked outside our code.”
  • “The logs weren’t enough, so I queried login success in the data platform.”
  • “The provider portal showed our DLT registration was blocked.”
  • “I shipped the fallback behind a feature flag and enabled it progressively per app.”
  • “Since then, provider delivery is logged, success-rate alerts fire, and the path is SMS → WhatsApp → in-app OTP.”

► Avoid this

  • “It was the vendor’s fault” — even if true, you own the system that depended on it.
  • “Users couldn’t log in for days” — don’t inflate; if it was long, explain what took time.
  • “Ops handled it / I just watched” — you were the owner, show yourself working.
  • “We had no monitoring” without immediately adding how you closed the gap.
  • Untranslated jargon (team names, internal tools) and precise revenue-loss or uptime figures you can’t actually stand behind.
  • Colleague or vendor names — keep it about the system.
Golden rule: one sentence of impact, three of method, two of system change. The interviewer should hear an owner who was calm, methodical, and made the system better afterwards.
